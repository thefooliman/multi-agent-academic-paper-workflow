## 1. Introduction

The grid was built on a simple assumption: generation follows demand. That
assumption is weakening. Wind and solar supply a growing share of electricity,
and their output tracks the weather rather than the clock. A still evening or a
cloudy afternoon removes power from the system at the moment it is needed most.

That is the problem this paper addresses. Variable renewable energy is not simply
less abundant than conventional power; it is less predictable. Operators have
always balanced supply and demand in real time, but variable sources make that
balance harder to hold. When the wind drops, something else must step in, and it
must step in quickly. The grid has long coped with demand spikes; it is far less
practiced at coping with supply that disappears.

Energy storage is the most commonly proposed answer. A battery can absorb surplus
generation when the sun is high and release it when the sun is gone; unlike a
thermal plant, it can respond within seconds, and in principle this turns
variable output into something closer to firm supply. Costs have fallen sharply
over the past decade, which has moved storage from a niche option to a serious
candidate in most planning debates.

We would not push that claim too far. Storage is not one technology but several,
and integration covers problems that range from second-by-second frequency
control to weather lulls that last for days. Lithium-ion batteries, pumped hydro,
and thermal storage share little beyond their function. What works at one
timescale may fail at another. The case for storage is compelling in theory and
still being tested in practice.

This paper examines that gap. We ask what energy storage can realistically do to
help renewable energy fit the grid, and where its limits lie. We treat storage as
a set of technologies rather than a single fix, and we weigh both its technical
contribution and its cost; the chapters that follow first review how variable
generation stresses the grid, then survey storage technologies and their
economics, and finally assess the evidence on whether storage delivers on its
promise.
